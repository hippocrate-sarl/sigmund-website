GDPR and patient records in Luxembourg

Obligations for psychiatrists, psychotherapists and psychologists in private practice

Psychiatrist Psychotherapist Psychologist

Mental health professionals handle some of the most sensitive personal data on a daily basis: diagnoses, session notes, prescriptions, medical history. Since the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, EU Regulation 2016/679) came into force on 25 May 2018, this data has been subject to strict rules, reinforced in Luxembourg by the law of 1 August 2018 establishing the Commission Nationale pour la Protection des Données (CNPD — Luxembourg's data protection authority).

Often overlooked by private practitioners, GDPR non-compliance can result in administrative fines of up to €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover (Art. 83.5 GDPR). This guide sets out your concrete obligations.

Mental health data: a specially protected category

Article 9 of the GDPR classifies health data as a "special category" of personal data. Such data is defined as "personal data relating to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveals information about his or her health status".

For a psychiatrist, psychotherapist or psychologist, this covers:

  • Psychiatric diagnoses (ICD-11 or DSM-5 codes)
  • Session notes and clinical summaries
  • Prescriptions and medical orders
  • Medical and family history
  • Psychological test results and assessments
  • Correspondence with other healthcare professionals
  • Billing data containing CNS medical procedure codes

Important: CNS billing data also qualifies as health data under the GDPR, because procedure codes indirectly reveal the nature of the care provided. It is subject to the same security requirements as the medical record itself.

The legal basis for processing: what do you need?

Processing health data is in principle prohibited, except in the cases exhaustively listed in Article 9.2 of the GDPR. For healthcare practitioners in private practice, two legal bases primarily apply:

  • Art. 9.2.h GDPR — medical purposes (diagnosis, treatment, care) carried out by a health professional bound by professional secrecy. This is the standard legal basis for maintaining a patient record.
  • Art. 9.2.a GDPR — explicit consent of the patient. Use with caution: consent must be freely given, informed, and revocable at any time without consequences for the patient's care.

For non-medical psychologists, the Luxembourg law of 26 July 2010 regulating the psychology profession, together with the guidelines of the Ordre des Psychologues du Luxembourg, specifies the conditions of practice and ethical obligations regarding patient records.

Retention periods: how long must you keep records?

The "storage limitation" principle (Art. 5.1.e GDPR) requires that data be kept no longer than necessary for the purpose for which it was collected. In practice in Luxembourg:

  • Psychiatrists: the Code of Ethics of the Luxembourg Medical Council (CMDL) requires a minimum retention period of 10 years after the last consultation.
  • Psychotherapists and psychologists: in the absence of a specific legal provision, a period of 10 years is recommended, consistent with the general ten-year prescription period under Luxembourg law.
  • Minors: retention is recommended until the patient reaches majority, plus the standard ten-year period.

Important distinction: the minimum retention period (a legal obligation) does not predetermine the maximum. Once the legal deadline has passed, data must be deleted or anonymised — unless a specific legal reason justifies extended retention.

Data security: your concrete obligations

Article 32 of the GDPR requires the implementation of appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk. For a mental health practice, this means:

  1. Data encryption — at rest (disks and databases) and in transit (HTTPS connections, secure messaging).
  2. Access control — strong passwords, two-factor authentication, access restricted to authorised personnel only.
  3. Secure hosting — using a provider with appropriate guarantees for health data. Sigmund hosts its data in Luxembourg with Gandi SAS (Bissen datacentre, Grand Duchy).
  4. Access logging — full traceability of consultations and modifications to patient records.
  5. Regular backups — tested and documented backup procedures, with off-site copies.
  6. Incident response plan — a defined procedure for personal data breaches (ransomware, device theft, unauthorised access).

Patient rights you must respect

The GDPR grants patients rights they can exercise at any time. You generally have one month to respond (Art. 12.3 GDPR):

  • Right of access (Art. 15) — the patient may obtain a copy of all their data and information about how it is processed.
  • Right to rectification (Art. 16) — correct inaccurate or incomplete data.
  • Right to erasure (Art. 17) — limited for medical records: the legal retention obligation takes precedence (Art. 17.3.b GDPR).
  • Right to data portability (Art. 20) — provide data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format.
  • Right to object (Art. 21) — only for processing based on legitimate interest or a public interest task; limited in practice for care records.

Transparency obligation (Art. 13 GDPR): at the first consultation, you must inform patients how their data is processed — identity of the data controller, purposes, retention periods, exercisable rights, and the CNPD's contact details. A signed intake document or a notice displayed in the waiting room is the recommended approach.

The record of processing activities: a little-known obligation

Article 30 of the GDPR requires every data controller — including a sole practitioner — to maintain a record of processing activities. This internal document must list:

  • Categories of data processed (health, administrative, financial)
  • Purposes of processing (care, billing, CNS communications)
  • Retention periods applied
  • Recipients of the data (CNS, insurers, colleagues, experts)
  • Security measures in place
  • Any transfers outside the EU

This record does not need to be submitted to the CNPD proactively, but must be produced on request. The CNPD provides record templates on its website.

Do you need to appoint a DPO (Data Protection Officer)?

Appointing a Data Protection Officer (DPO) is mandatory in three cases under Article 37 of the GDPR:

  • Public authorities and bodies
  • Controllers carrying out large-scale regular and systematic monitoring of individuals
  • Large-scale processing of special categories of data (including health data)

For a practitioner working alone or in a small group, the concept of "large scale" generally rules out the obligation. The CNPD nonetheless recommends a case-by-case assessment, particularly for multi-practitioner mental health centres. When in doubt, voluntarily appointing a DPO is good practice.

Notifying the CNPD of a data breach

In the event of a personal data breach — cyberattack, stolen computer, unauthorised access, accidental disclosure to a third party — Article 33 of the GDPR requires you to notify the CNPD within 72 hours. If the breach is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of the patients concerned, they must also be notified directly (Art. 34 GDPR).

Notifications are submitted online via the CNPD portal (cnpd.public.lu). Failure to notify, or exceeding the 72-hour deadline, itself constitutes a breach that may be subject to sanctions.

Key figures: according to the CNPD's 2023 annual report, data breaches in the healthcare sector are among the most frequently reported categories in Luxembourg. The medical sector is a prime target for cyberattacks given the high value of health data on illegal markets.

Frequently asked questions about GDPR in mental health practice

Can a patient demand deletion of their medical record?
Generally no, if retention is required by law (10 years after the last consultation for physicians). Article 17.3.b GDPR expressly provides that the right to erasure does not apply where processing is necessary to comply with a legal obligation. However, if data is retained beyond the legal deadline or for non-medical purposes, the right to erasure applies in full.
Can I send medical documents to a patient by standard email?
Unencrypted email does not constitute a secure channel under Article 32 GDPR. Sending health data via ordinary email creates a risk of a data breach. It is recommended to use end-to-end encrypted messaging, or at a minimum to send sensitive attachments as password-protected files, communicating the password through a separate channel.
Does GDPR apply to my practice if I only work in Luxembourg?
Yes, without exception. The GDPR applies to any processing of data belonging to EU residents, regardless of the size of the organisation or its turnover. A sole practitioner is fully subject to this regulation. There is no minimum threshold that exempts a medical or psychology practice from its fundamental obligations.
Do I need to inform patients about how I use their data?
Yes, this is a legal obligation (Art. 13 GDPR). This information must be provided at the time of first data collection, typically at the first consultation. It can take the form of a document signed by the patient, a notice displayed in the waiting room, or a mention in the intake form. It must cover the identity of the data controller, processing purposes, retention periods, exercisable rights, and the CNPD's contact details.
Is CNS billing data subject to GDPR in the same way as medical records?
Yes. CNS billing data contains procedure codes that reveal the nature of the care provided. It is therefore considered health data under Article 9 GDPR and subject to the same security requirements, retention rules and patient rights as the medical record itself.
What are the practical consequences of non-compliance?
The CNPD can issue warnings, compliance orders, temporary processing bans and administrative fines. For the most serious infringements (Art. 83.5 GDPR), fines can reach €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover. In practice, sanctions for private practitioners are proportionate to the severity of the breach — but the reputational impact and loss of patient trust are also significant risks.
